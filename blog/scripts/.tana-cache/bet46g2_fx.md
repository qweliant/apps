          - In 1962 Engelbart wrote up [Augmenting Human Intelligence](tana:PWY7jBEW3Ght) was the first summary of the philosophy behind the experiments at XPARC. If memex was a fantastical proposal for knowledge work then AHI was the formal requirements gathering for implementing such systems. It was a blueprint for reshaping cognition through machines. He imagined tools that could scale our ability to solve complex problems through feedback loops, linked knowledge, and multi-user collaboration. His work anticipated the development of hypertext, envisioning systems where information could be linked and accessed non-linearly, a precursor to the modern web. This culminated in the Xerox Alto presented in the mother of all demos in 1968, then in 72' the Xerox Alito, using the [NLS](tana:yd-E31Us6p74) system Engelbart and the team at PARC created.   - Interconnected Thinking — Rewrite v3 <!-- node-id: hGvVOiCvLRW9 -->
    - In 1945 Vannevar Bush published *As We May Think* in The Atlantic. He'd spent his career inside institutions of higher learning — running MIT's engineering school, then Carnegie — and by 1939 was operating inside government-funded military research. By '42 he was the organizational head of the Manhattan Project. In '45, after organizing some of the brightest minds to build the most violent machines, grandaddy Bush distilled his vision of computationally-aided design into the Memex: a desk-sized device that would store and retrieve knowledge in ways that mimicked the associative nature of thought and sense. It was the grandaddy article. Everything that came next was arguing with it. Fifteen years later J.C.R. Licklider turned the vision into a design agenda. *Man-Computer Symbiosis* (1960) proposed that humans set goals and evaluate; computers do the clerical work via tight real-time coupling. Licklider named the prerequisites — time-sharing, associative memory, natural I/O — and then he and Wes Clark started prototyping them. *On-Line Man-Computer Communication* (1962) reports on Tutor 1, Coplanner, and Program Graph: actual running systems that proved symbiosis was an engineering problem, not a metaphor. By 1963 Licklider was circulating the Intergalactic Computer Network memo to ARPA, a proto-ARPANET document that already named the coordination problems a network would have to solve. Intergalactic, you and me, bih. <!-- node-id: r3P2GIELgF3K -->
    - Douglas Engelbart ran with it. *Augmenting Human Intellect* (1962) was the formal requirements gathering for what Memex had only dreamed — a blueprint for reshaping cognition through feedback loops, linked knowledge, and multi-user collaboration. In 1968 he delivered the Mother of All Demos: NLS, the mouse, videoconferencing, shared documents, hypertext. By '72 PARC was building the Alto around those ideas, with their own local area networks putting them in the path of the coming protocol fights. In a quieter corner of the same timeline, John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz were trying to make programming accessible — BASIC was designed on the idea that computers didn't have to be terrifying. Gates and Allen rewrote it for the Altair; it eventually showed up in the Lisa Two and early Visual Basic. A language designed for classrooms ended up bootstrapping a generation of developers. Until the Raspberry Pi era, BASIC was the last glimpse of what hardware computing could be when people cared about access. And then there was Ted Nelson. In 1965, in *Complex Information Processing: A File Structure for the Complex, the Changing and the Indeterminate*, he coined hypertext and sketched a vast interlinked library users could explore, annotate, and modify. Xanadu wasn't a document viewer; it was an ownership protocol. Built-in identity and privacy. Copyright-respecting transclusion so quotations pointed back to originals. Bidirectional links, so looking at a piece of content let you find everything that cited it. Deep versioning so edits didn't destroy history. Micropayments so authors got paid when their work got reused. Nelson specified, in 1965, the ownership primitives the web still doesn't have. Alan Kay at PARC was doing the parallel work in a different register — Smalltalk, Dynabook, and much later the Croquet Project, which kept shipping prototypes of the ownership-first web into the 2010s. The vision wasn't refuted. It was unfunded. Real light-side-of-the-force shit. <!-- node-id: wQdAKJbgelsy -->
    - And then the defeat. By the late '80s the network capability of the world was catching up to the commercialization of PCs Jobs had hardware-capitalized and Gates had softwared. What we got was the Protocol Wars — TCP/IP against CYCLADES out of France, against INWG's internetworking proposal, against carrier-backed X.25, against the whole OSI stack. TCP/IP won for reasons that looked technical at the time and look political now. James Pelkey's long-form history walks the receipts. The end-to-end principle (Saltzer, Reed, Clark, 1984) became doctrine: keep the network dumb, put application logic at the endpoints. Postel's Law — "be conservative in what you send, liberal in what you accept" — selected for robustness over accountability. Compatible traffic wins; provenance loses. Alexander Galloway's point in *Protocol* (2004) reads as the quiet subtitle of this whole period: decentralization isn't the opposite of control, it's another form of it. Ted Nelson was seriously in his flop era. In 1989 at CERN, Tim Berners-Lee wrote *Information Management: A Proposal* — a system for interconnected knowledge management built on TCP/IP. Hypertext, hypermedia, decentralized, tags to encapsulate whatever you wanted to express. The proposal is explicit about the trade: TBL chose unidirectional, break-tolerant links over Xanadu's bidirectional ownership primitives because CERN needed something deployable. The architectural choice is stated in the text. Mark McCahill's Gopher, out of Minnesota in 1991, was the contemporaneous winner until Minnesota announced licensing fees in 1993 and institutions stampeded to HTTP. Gopher didn't lose on the merits. It lost on governance. Apple had already shipped AppleTalk in 1985 with auto-configuration and naming primitives TCP/IP didn't carry — vendor stacks solving usability problems the open standard had punted, which is also why the PC-era endpoint got commoditized under vendor lock instead of interop. Everything the pioneers wanted at the endpoint — identity, versioning, provenance, payment — either lived in proprietary silos or didn't exist at all. The web that shipped handled documents. It did not handle ownership. We're still paying that bill. <!-- node-id: -bnISgsHVJXN -->
    - In 1999 Nelson wrote *Xanalogical Structure, Needed Now More Than Ever* — a forty-year retrospective on exactly what the web broke. Asked about implementing the full vision he told an interviewer "I don't want to talk about the web today. I hate it." Project Xanadu became the longest-running vaporware in computing history, and the reason it stayed vaporware is that implementing it would have required changing the internet. The consequence is that knowledge graphs — the thing the web was meant to be, semantically, from the beginning — had to get built inside corporate firewalls. The engineering survey literature is unsubtle about this. Heist et al. (2020) compare public graphs (DBpedia, Wikidata) to proprietary ones (Google, Facebook) and the proprietary ones are an order of magnitude larger. Yan et al. (2018) detail why ontology alignment at web scale is expensive enough that nobody would do it in public. By 2020 Google's Knowledge Graph held 500 billion facts on 5 billion entities and mediated roughly a third of Google's 100 billion monthly searches. A single proprietary graph answers a third of humanity's questions — because the protocol layer was never going to. Lessig had the vocabulary for this as early as 1999: code is law. The web's silence on provenance is not an oversight. It's legislation-by-omission. <!-- node-id: 1lNapgj7UMmt -->
    - You can watch what happens when somebody tries to retrofit ownership at the application layer. Linden Lab shipped Second Life in 2003 with Linden Dollars, a real exchange rate, and retained IP for creators — the most honest experiment in bolting Xanadu primitives onto a post-web platform. It collapsed into governance crises, banking failures, and platform-controlled rollbacks. Peter Ludlow's *Alphaville Herald* and *Second Life Herald* documented EA and Linden Lab banning journalists, seizing accounts, and punishing critical coverage a decade before Facebook made the playbook household. Application-layer ownership isn't sufficient. You need the protocol layer underneath to carry it. We don't have that layer. We never built it. <!-- node-id: 2BCFfkQDYI2Q -->
    - That's the architectural question this essay is about. Not "is the web bad," not "is Google evil" — the structural one. The web's immaturity isn't an accident. It's the shape of every decision stacked above: ARPANET funding physics, the end-to-end principle, the OSI fight, the PC wars, CERN's deployment pressure, Minnesota's license fee, the end of Xerox's research era. Each one individually defensible. Together, a forty-year answer to Bush's question that wasn't the answer Bush asked for. The web should have been weird and interoperable. Instead it became uniform and commercial, and the browser wars didn't just kill off competition — they set the tone for how we'd interact with knowledge itself: through branded, sandboxed portals, not associative thought.
